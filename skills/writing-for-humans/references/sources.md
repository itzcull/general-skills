# Sources

This file records the evidence used to design this skill. The authoritative source packet reports live retrieval on 2026-09-15. Organizational style guidance remains attributed to its publisher and is not presented as a universal rule for every audience, language, or genre.

## H1 — Digital.gov: Principles of plain language

**Source:** [Principles of plain language](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/principles)

Used for:

- writing for a specific audience rather than a fixed grade level;
- accounting for reader knowledge, expertise, interests, and goals;
- topic sentences, active voice, organized information, summaries, lists, and tables.

**Implementation recommendation:** Apply these audience-first principles when local requirements do not conflict.

## H2 — Digital.gov: Writing for understanding

**Source:** [Writing for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/writing)

Used for:

- short words and sections;
- active voice and explicit responsibility;
- present tense when accurate;
- strong verbs instead of nominalizations.

**Qualification:** These are defaults for clarity, not absolute grammatical rules.

## H3 — Digital.gov: Design for understanding

**Source:** [Design for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/design)

Used for:

- key information at the top;
- descriptive, hierarchical headings;
- lists for scanning and tables for relationships;
- restrained emphasis and the risks of all caps or underlining non-links;
- accessible implementation of structural elements.

**Implementation recommendation:** Also follow the accessibility and publishing requirements of the target medium.

## H4 — Digital.gov: Test for understanding

**Source:** [Test for understanding](https://digital.gov/guides/plain-language/test)

Used for:

- testing during writing rather than only after completion;
- early, iterative testing and revision;
- paraphrase, usability, and comparative testing;
- using focus groups for attitudes rather than proof of comprehension.

**Implementation recommendation:** Make testing effort proportionate to risk, reach, and access to representative readers.

## H5 — W3C cognitive accessibility guidance

**Source:** [Making Content Usable for People with Cognitive and Learning Disabilities](https://www.w3.org/TR/coga-usable/)

**Status:** W3C Working Group Note providing supplemental guidance beyond WCAG requirements.

Used for:

- clear headings, boundaries, familiar terms, and consistent patterns;
- common words, short sentences and blocks, simple tense, active voice, and literal language;
- one topic per paragraph, one point per sentence, summaries, and separated instructions;
- avoiding double negatives, nested clauses, unexplained jargon, memory dependence, and dense pages;
- testing tasks with diverse real users rather than relying only on automation.

**Qualification:** This note is not a WCAG conformance requirement. Its patterns support cognitive accessibility but do not certify comprehension for every user.

## H6 — Google developer documentation style guide

**Source:** [Google developer documentation style guide: Highlights](https://developers.google.com/style/highlights)

Used for:

- conversational but credible tone;
- accessibility and global audiences;
- second person, active voice, and conditions before instructions;
- sentence-case headings, numbered sequences, bullets, descriptive links, unambiguous dates, and image alt text.

**Qualification:** This is Google's documentation style. Apply it when suitable for the audience and local style guide rather than as a universal rule.

## H7 — Diátaxis

**Source:** [Diátaxis](https://diataxis.fr/)

Used for distinguishing documentation that teaches, enables a task, supports lookup, or explains: tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation.

**Source scope:** Diátaxis is a documentation architecture, not a classification of every kind of human-facing writing.

**Implementation recommendation:** Small documents may combine forms in clearly separated sections.
